The story: one wind shift, an airport-wide consequence

Imagine your aircraft is ready to leave Frankfurt. The wind shifts. Across the airport, the operating direction may need to change. Handling teams reposition, departure plans change, and connections come under pressure. FraAlliance described roughly three direction changes a week. This is an airport-wide planning problem.

2:00–2:35

What went into the model

We combined public records of the operating direction actually flown with Frankfurt METAR observations and archived forecasts from ECMWF, GFS and ICON. Pressure differences around Frankfurt add information about approaching weather. The direction archive reaches back to 2018, but the joined forecast training data starts in 2024. Flight schedules and US airline-delay data are not model inputs.

SHOW DFLD + METAR + archived ECMWF/GFS/ICON + pressure → features → trees + persistence → hourly forecast.

2:35–3:10

How it works and what changed

Two gradient-boosted tree classifiers estimate operating direction and runway-18 usability. Near-term direction forecasts blend with the current state, with weights selected on earlier validation data. On the same twelve evaluation months, separate weather-model features improved forty-eight-hour accuracy from 84.9 to 88.6 percent. Adding pressure features brought it to 89.2. Deeper trees and doubling the issue frequency did not help, so we kept the simpler version.

SHOW Same 12 folds, 48 h: base feature set 84.9% → separate weather models 88.6% → v5 with pressure 89.2%.

3:10–3:50

How we tested it

For each test month, we trained on earlier months and compared all methods on the same scored rows. We used archived forecasts with an assumed six-hour publication lag, rather than future observations. At one hour the methods effectively tie. At six, twenty-four and forty-eight hours, the model improves on both headline baselines. Day-resampled confidence intervals support those margins. Feature selection did look at these folds, however, so a later, frozen test is still needed.

SHOW Train earlier months → test next month, repeated Nov 2025–Oct 2026. Show model/rules/persistence table at 1/6/24/48 h.

3:50–4:25

What the score does not promise

Direction accuracy is not exact switch-time accuracy. The next change falls within three hours in about half the evaluated change windows. False alarms occur in about thirteen percent of no-change windows. Runway-18 labels are inferred from weather, and positive cases are rare: at twenty-four hours the model detects only three of twenty-four positive issue windows. This is a planning prototype with clear limits.

SHOW Timing ±3 h: 52.5% of change windows. False alarms: 12.8% of no-change windows. Runway 18: 3/24 positive issue windows detected at 24 h.

4:25–5:00

The pilot we want

A pilot needs the airport’s own timestamped direction and closure records, real flight demand and delay causes. We would freeze the model, record every prediction before its outcome, and measure whether earlier warnings improve preparation after accounting for false alarms and action costs. The ambition is shared planning across the airport. Today, we can demonstrate the forecast and the evidence behind its measured improvement.

2:00–3:00 · shorter backup

One minute of proof

We trained gradient-boosted trees on recorded directions, METAR and archived weather forecasts. Twelve monthly walk-forward folds compare the model with the challenge rules and keeping the current direction. One hour is a tie; at twenty-four hours, accuracy is 91.7 percent versus 87.7 for the rules. But these folds informed feature selection, so we still need a frozen prospective test. Timing is within three hours in only about half of change windows, and runway-18 closure detection remains weak. Our next step is a pilot with airport records and demand data, measuring planning benefit rather than assuming savings.
