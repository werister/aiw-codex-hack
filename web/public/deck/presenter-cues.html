SLIDE 2 · 0:15–0:31

The planning problem

When the operating direction changes, people and equipment need to be coordinated. A shift lead needs to know which configuration to prepare for, when it may change, and how uncertain the forecast is.

SHOW Direction change and ground-handling context. The airport animation illustrates the setting; it is not measured traffic or a delay simulation.

SLIDE 3 · 0:31–0:46

The challenge baseline

Our baseline applies the challenge wind limits to forecast weather. We also compare with keeping the current direction. These are reproducible reference methods, not a description of every tool the airport already uses.

SLIDE 4 · 0:46–1:06

The model

The model combines forecast wind, the challenge rules and recent recorded conditions. It learns patterns with boosted trees and blends direction forecasts with the current state. We compare it with applying the wind rules directly and simply keeping the current direction.

SHOW One tree from the published model. Describe the method without reading every split.

SLIDE 5 · 1:06–1:31

What we built

There are two forecast targets: the operating direction, west or east, and runway-eighteen weather usability. Each hour also has a certainty score, and we show the next likely direction change. These are planning inputs. DFS retains the operating decision.

SLIDE 6 · 1:31–1:51

The data

We combine public recorded directions from DFLD, airport weather observations from IEM, and archived forecasts from Open-Meteo. The direction archive reaches back to 2018; the joined forecast inputs start in 2024. These public records are proxies for operations, not certified movement logs.

SHOW Recorded-direction chart and its source block. Keep source attribution visible.

SLIDE 9 · 2:26–2:51

The measured result

At twenty-four hours, the model gets the direction right in 91.8 percent of scored hours, versus 87.7 for the challenge wind rules and 75.1 for keeping the current direction. At one hour, keeping the current direction is slightly better. These scores measure direction, not exact change timing.

SHOW Results at 1, 6 and 24 hours. Point to the 24-hour comparison; the 48-hour extension is secondary.

SLIDE 10 · 2:51–3:06

Change timing

Direction accuracy does not guarantee exact change timing. The separate event evaluation matches 115 of 193 recorded changes within three hours, with 138 false-alarm clusters. That limits how warnings should be used.

SHOW Distinct-event result and false-alarm count together. Do not describe the separate change-subset hourly accuracy as event detection.

SLIDE 11 · 3:06–3:21

Runway 18 and certainty

Runway eighteen remains weak: no warnings in twelve positive twenty-four-hour windows. The labels are inferred from weather, not actual closures. Our certainty score is not a calibrated chance of being correct.

SHOW Zero of twelve positive 24-hour windows; 307 complete windows and 1,125 exclusions. These are overlapping windows, not twelve independent closures.
