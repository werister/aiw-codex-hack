What We Found 

BTS relies on informal procedures to verify the accuracy of on-time

performance data and lacks procedures to verify the data’s completeness. 

•   BTS has established informal quality control procedures to identify

inaccuracies in on-time performance data submitted by reporting carriers.

For example, BTS developed an automated process that performs 117

automated checks of the data to identify any errors or inconsistencies. 

•   However, BTS has not yet formally adopted these procedures nor

established a plan to ensure their continued execution. 

•   Although BTS requires reporting carriers to certify that the on-time

performance information they submit is correct and complete, the

Agency does not have a method for verifying that carriers are reporting

data for all flights, raising concerns about the data’s reliability. 

BTS lacks effective guidance and procedures for verifying reported causes

data. 

•   BTS’ technical directives, which provide guidance to carriers on reporting

the causes of flight delays and cancellations, lack clarity, leading air

carriers to interpret the guidance and report their data inconsistently. 

•   BTS lacks procedures to identify and address inconsistencies in reported

causes data. Instead, the Agency relies on methods the air carriers are

using to ensure their submitted data is accurate. 

•   We found a high rate of disagreement between data from FAA on

reported causes versus the data that carriers report to BTS. BTS has not

taken steps to understand and address the causes of these discrepancies.

Background 

BTS is one of 13 principal Federal statistical agencies and is the source of

statistics on commercial aviation, multimodal freight activity, and transportation

economics. Its work provides context to decision makers and the public for

understanding statistics on transportation. BTS’ mission is to assure the credibility

of its products and services through rigorous analysis, transparent data quality,

and independence from political influence. BTS aims to promote innovative

methods of data collection, analysis, visualization, and dissemination to improve

operational efficiency, examine emerging topics, and create relevant and timely

information products that foster understanding of transportation and its

transformational role in society. As a principal Federal statistical agency, BTS is

governed primarily by statute 
10   
and statistical policy directives of the Office of

Management and Budget. 

In November 2002, DOT issued its regulation requiring reporting carriers to

collect and report the causes of flight delays and cancellations to BTS. The

reported causes for flight delays are categorized in five broad categories:

circumstances within the airline's control (e.g., lack of flight crew,

maintenance, aircraft cleaning, baggage loading, fueling, etc.).

forecasted) that, in the judgment of the carrier, delays or prevents the

operation of a flight such as thunderstorm, deicing aircraft, tornado,

hurricane, or snowstorm.

that refer to a broad set of conditions, such as non-extreme weather

conditions

control.

causing the present flight to depart late.

concourse, reboarding of aircraft because of security breach, inoperative

BTS Relies on Informal Procedures To Verify the 

Accuracy of On-Time Performance Data and Lacks

Procedures To Verify the Data’s Completeness 

BTS has established informal quality control procedures to identify inaccuracies in

on-time performance data submitted by reporting carriers. However, BTS has not

yet formally adopted these procedures. Moreover, BTS has not established a plan

to ensure the continued execution of its quality assurance procedures. Finally,

BTS does not have a method for verifying that carriers are reporting data for all

flights. 

BTS Has Procedures To Verify the 

Accuracy of On-Time Performance Data,

but It Has Not Formally Adopted Them 

BTS has developed quality control procedures that allow it to identify and resolve

inaccuracies in the on-time performance data it receives from reporting carriers.

However, the Agency has not formally adopted these procedures.

receives from reporting carriers. DOT’s regulation requires reporting carriers to

submit their on-time performance data to BTS within 15 days following the end

of the month. BTS requires that carriers use the format prescribed in its Form 234,

BTS Has Not Formally Adopted Its Procedures for Verifying

Data Accuracy 

Although BTS has a process for identifying inaccuracies in on-time performance

data, as described above, the Agency has not yet formally adopted these

procedures. In May 2023, after OIG initiated its audit, BTS developed draft

guidance that documents the Agency’s current quality assurance process for

collecting, validating, and disseminating airline-reported Form 234 data.

However, since that time, the Agency has not updated its draft standard

operating procedures (SOPs) 
20   
to reflect this guidance or formally adopted the

SOPs. According to BTS officials, even though the guidance has not been formally

adopted, the Agency is following the quality assurance process, and since the

draft quality assurance processes serve as internal guidance only, they have not

been officially adopted. 

After our review of the 2023 draft operating procedures, a senior OAI official

provided SOPs BTS developed in 2009 for our review to demonstrate the Agency

had longstanding policies in place. However, these SOPs are inadequate. First, the

SOPs are outdated and do not cover the Agency’s current quality assurance

process for collecting, processing, reviewing, and reporting airline reported Form

234 data. Second, the SOPs are in draft and have never been formally adopted by

the Agency. Finally, the existence of these SOPs was not common knowledge

within the Agency, as reflected by the fact that the BTS staff we interacted with

throughout the audit, including the BTS Data Administrator, were not aware of

the 2009 SOPs’ existence. 

The Federal statute that established BTS states that the Director of BTS shall

encourage the standardization of data, data collection methods, and data

management and storage technologies for data collected by the Agency. Also,

BTS’ Statistical Standards Manual (statistical manual) 
21   
states that data processing

procedures must be documented, and that the Agency’s data may be subjected

to an external peer review, which would require the Agency to have documented

procedures. Additionally, the manual establishes professional standards to ensure

consistency among studies conducted by statistical agencies to provide users

clear documentation of the methods used to collect, process, analyze, and

disseminate statistical information. 

Without having adopted formal written procedures, BTS runs the risk of

procedures not being consistently followed or properly executed, particularly if

the Agency must rely on new employees or personnel unfamiliar with the quality

assurance process. Additionally, the lack of formal documented procedures limits

BTS Lacks Effective Guidance and Procedures for 

Verifying Reported Causes Data 

BTS’ technical directives, which provide guidance to carriers on reporting the

causes of flight delays and cancellations, lack clarity, resulting in carriers

interpreting the guidance and reporting their data inconsistently. Furthermore,

BTS lacks procedures to identify and address inconsistencies in reported causes

data. 

BTS’ Unclear Guidance Results in Airlines 

Reporting Cause Data Inconsistently 

DOT’s regulation requires reporting carriers to collect and report the causes of

airline delays and cancellations. BTS’ technical directives, which are issued

annually, provide guidance to reporting carriers on how they should collect and

report their Form 234 data to provide a better understanding of the causes of

delays and cancellations. However, our review has found that unclear guidance in

BTS’ technical directives has resulted in carriers’ interpreting the guidance

inconsistently. As a result, some carriers submit reporting cause data differently

than others, which introduces inconsistencies into the data reported to BTS. 

Specifically, in the same technical directive, BTS states that carriers have the

option of reporting multiple causes of departure delays or the predominant

cause of departure delays. However, the technical directive does not sufficiently

clarify that a carrier can only choose the predominant cause to report departure

delays “when a delay cause has fewer than 6 minutes of delays.” DOT’s regulation

states that “reporting carriers are required to code delays only when the arrival

delay is 15 minutes or greater; and reporting carriers must report each causal

component of the reportable delay when the causal component is 5 minutes or

greater.” While BTS’ technical directive 
29   
does reiterate this regulatory

requirement, it further adds to inconsistent reporting because the directive asks 

29  BTS’ technical directive #27, issued on August 15, 2017.

that carriers account for departure delays of 6 minutes or longer, instead of the

regulation’s requirement to report departure delays of 5 minutes or longer. 

BTS’ unclear guidance has resulted in some carriers submitting their reporting

cause data differently than others. For example, some carriers are grouping and

reporting all minutes of delays under one predominant cause. Furthermore,

because BTS’ guidance does not define what the Agency means by predominant

cause or how carriers should use it to report their data, carriers have used their

judgement differently in interpreting how and when to report a predominant

cause. One of the six carriers we spoke with told us that that they interpreted the

predominant cause to mean the delay cause with the longest duration. Another

carrier told us that they interpreted the predominant cause to mean the root

cause of the delay. In all, the six airlines in our sample are using three different

methodologies in providing their reported causes data to BTS: (1) two carriers use

multiple causes, (2) three use predominant cause by longest duration, and (3) one

uses predominant cause by root cause. 

Effectively, the technical directive allows for multiple interpretations of delay

events. By allowing multiple interpretations, the guidance creates opportunities

for an inconsistent and incomplete understanding of the causes of delays and

cancellations. Moreover, these data inconsistencies make it difficult to compare

the operational performance of one carrier to another carrier who may have used

a different methodology to report their data. In one instance, we came across a

carrier who switched from using the multiple causes approach to report their

data to the predominant cause approach. In this case, the resulting inconsistency

in the data would make it difficult to compare that carrier’s operating

performance from before they switched their reporting methodology to after

they switched. Therefore, any analysis of inconsistent data could lead to an

unreliable assessment of problems as well as the actions taken to address them. 

According to several BTS officials, the reason for the wording in the technical

directives being unclear is unknown. The officials agreed that the lack of clarity

results in inconsistencies in the data collected and distributed by BTS, which

raises concerns about the data’s reliability.

Address Inconsistencies in Reported

Causes Data 

The certification statement that reporting carriers submit with their Form 234

data each month also covers reported causes data. However, outside of this

requirement, BTS has not developed any other quality control procedures to

verify the consistency of carrier-submitted reported causes data. As a result, the

and cancellations. All air carriers we interviewed told us that having access to

inefficiencies in their operations and develop a more targeted response to

alleviate those inefficiencies. Additionally, the carriers generally assign the

responsibility for managing different causal categories to different individuals or

groups, which creates an incentive to ensure that any inconsistencies in assigning

delay codes are identified and addressed. All six carriers we spoke with have also

established a process to research and arbitrate any disagreements between

departments. In some cases, carriers have also established an internal audit

function to ensure that the carrier is capturing delay or cancellation causes under

the appropriate categories. The carriers we spoke with told us that they have also

developed custom methodologies to consolidate the granular data into the four

or five categories that are captured in their Form 234 data and reported to BTS. 

Based on our discussions with these six carriers, we were able to determine that

they were using three different methodologies to report their causal data,

resulting in inconsistencies in the data. However, BTS has not recognized the

inconsistencies between the carriers’ approaches as an issue, and therefore has

not developed procedures to identify and reconcile such inconsistencies.

Conclusion 

Flight delays and cancellations are prevalent in the aviation industry. Collecting

reliable delay and cancellation data is critical to understanding and addressing

these events. BTS takes several steps to verify the accuracy of on-time

performance data it receives from reporting carriers. However, the Agency has

not yet formalized its processes or established procedures to ensure carriers are

reporting complete and consistent delays and cancellation data. By improving

consistency in reported cause data, DOT will be better positioned to evaluate

carriers’ operating and scheduling performance and to take actions to benefit

airline customers or improve the efficiency of the NAS. 

Recommendations 

To improve the completeness and consistency of on-time performance and

reported causes data, we recommend that the Director of the Bureau of

Transportation Statistics (BTS): 

1.   Update and formalize the Agency’s standard operating procedures for

collecting, processing, and disseminating airline on-time performance data

and make sure that these procedures are disseminated internally and

available to all relevant staff. 

2.   Conduct an assessment of whether the BTS Data Administrator position and

any other position should be designated as mission critical. 

3.   Develop succession plans for any mission-critical positions. 

4.   Develop and implement a process to verify that carriers are reporting on-time

performance data for all flights. 

5.   Evaluate the Agency’s technical directives to identify areas where it could

provide clarifying or additional guidance to promote consistency in the data

reported by carriers. 

6.   Work with FAA to understand no-matches in the National Airspace System

(NAS) delay data between BTS’ Airline Service Quality Performance (ASQP)

and FAA’s Aviation System Performance Metrics (ASPM) databases; and

improve the consistency of air carrier-reported NAS delay data by providing

additional carrier guidance as applicable. 

7.   Develop and implement a process to assess the consistency across air

carriers’ reporting of causes of delay data.

This performance audit was conducted between February 2023 and August 2024.

We conducted this audit in accordance with generally accepted Government

auditing standards. Those standards require that we plan and perform the audit

to obtain sufficient, appropriate evidence to provide a reasonable basis for our

findings and conclusions based on our audit objectives. We believe that the

evidence obtained provides a reasonable basis for our findings and conclusions

based on our audit objectives. 

Initially, our audit objectives were to (1) identify the reported causes of flight

delays and cancellations and (2) assess the controls DOT has in place to ensure

the completeness and accuracy of reported flight delay and cancellation data.

However, due to the breadth of data collected by both FAA and BTS on flight

delays and cancellations, in November 2023, we revised our audit objective to

focus on the reliability of the flight delay and cancellation data collected by BTS.

Specifically, our revised audit objective was to assess the controls BTS has in

place to ensure the completeness and accuracy of on-time performance and

reported causes data. 

We reviewed applicable laws, regulations, and BTS policies and procedures,

including BTS’ Statistical Standards Manual, technical directives, and SOPs

governing the Agency’s collecting and processing of flight delay and cancellation

data. We interviewed officials from BTS’ OAI and Office of Transportation

Analysis, FAA, and OACP. We also sampled 6 out of the 17 reporting carriers in

calendar year 2022. Our sample was selected based on a simple random sample

that was stratified by the type of reporting carrier: Legacy, Low-Cost Carrier and

Ultra Low-Cost Carrier, and Regional. Our sample of six reporting carriers

included two carriers from each stratum. 

In our review of domestic scheduled air carrier flight delays and cancellations

Subject:   Management Response to Office of Inspector General (OIG) Draft Report: 

The Bureau of Transportation Statistics Verifies the Accuracy of Flight

Delay and Cancellation Data but Can Do More To Assess Its

Completeness and Consistency 

The Bureau of Transportation Statistics (BTS), part of the Department of Transportation (DOT),

is the preeminent source of statistics on commercial aviation. BTS analyzes systemwide and

carrier-specific delay and cancellation data collected by its Office of Airline Information to

understand the nature and causes of flight delays and cancellations. BTS strives to ensure the

completeness and accuracy of data for on-time performance and reported causes of delays and

cancellations by working with carriers to resolve inconsistencies with air traffic control data and

publishing corrections when carriers discover problems with their submissions. Further, BTS

works extensively with the Federal Aviation Administration to analyze and understand

discrepancies between delays that are attributed to the National Airspace System verses delays

reported by air carriers. 

BTS continuously looks for opportunities to assess and adjust its processes to enhance product

quality, manage risks, and improve efficiency of the data it publishes to the public. The OIG

report correctly notes that BTS causal data on delays and cancellations is used by the

Department’s Office of Aviation Consumer Protection (OACP), a unit within the Office of the

General Counsel. However, the statements in the report that inconsistencies in this data impacts

OACP’s understanding of the nature and causes of flight delays and cancellations and the

effectiveness of the actions it takes to improve air travel are inaccurate. As noted elsewhere in

the report, the BTS data is only one of several tools used to monitor and understand the nature

and causes of flight delays and cancellations.
