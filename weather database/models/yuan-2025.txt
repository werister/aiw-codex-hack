Abstract 

Accurate prediction of airport delays under convective weather conditions is essential for

effective traffic coordination and improving overall airport efficiency. Traditional methods

mainly rely on numerical weather and traffic indicators, but they often fail to capture the

spatial distribution of traffic flows within the terminal area. To address this limitation, we

propose a novel image-based representation named Multi-Channel Traffic and Weather

Scene Image (MTWSI), which maps both meteorological and traffic information onto a

two-dimensional airspace grid, thereby preserving spatial relationships. Based on the

MTWSI, we develop a delay prediction model named ADLCNN. This model first uses a

convolutional neural network to extract deep spatial features from the scene images and

then classifies each sample into a delay level. Using real operational data from Guangzhou

Baiyun Airport, this paper shows that ADLCNN achieves significantly higher prediction ac-

curacy compared to traditional machine learning methods. The results confirm that MTWSI

provides a more accurate representation of real traffic conditions under convective weather. 

Keywords:   airport delay prediction; image recognition; convolutional neural network

(CNN); convective weather; class imbalance 

1. Introduction 

Airport delay serves as a key indicator of an airport’s overall operational performance,

reflecting its efficiency and the effectiveness of flight scheduling. Various factors, such as air

traffic control, weather conditions, aircraft maintenance, flight scheduling, airport facilities,

and human factors, work together to cause airport delays. Serious delays can lead to flight

congestion, a higher risk of conflicts or accidents, and major inconvenience for airlines,

passengers, and airport operations. To reduce these negative effects, intelligent airport

delay prediction systems have emerged as a key part of Intelligent Air Traffic Management

(IATM). These systems aim to improve operational efficiency by using Artificial Intelligence

(AI) for real-time monitoring and early warnings of flight delays.

The impact of weather on airport delays is particularly pronounced, especially when

convective weather in terminal areas, such as high winds, hailstorms, or thunderstorms,

disrupts aircraft operations and ground equipment. Therefore, an effective airport delay

prediction system must take weather conditions into account. The primary objective of

this paper is to characterize the influence of terminal area convective weather on flight

operations by employing a multi-channel pictorial method that accurately depicts the

2. Literature Review 

2.1. Related Work 

2.1.1. Research on Weather Delay Index

Since the late 1990s, airport delay studies in the context of severe weather have been

at the forefront of research, with pioneering work led by organizations such as MITRE,

MIT Lincoln Laboratory, and NASA. Early studies focused on the Weather Impacted Index

(WITI) developed by MITRE, along with related initiatives like MITRE’s Flow Contingency

Management (FCM), the application of Ensemble Weather Forecasts (EWF) to quantify

weather uncertainty, and the FAA-led Air Traffic Management–Weather Integration study.

In terms of WITI, a great deal of research has been done by scholars at home and

abroad. Callaham et al. [1] introduced WITI in 2001 to quantify how unfavorable weather

affects the National Airspace System, particularly for large-scale or long-duration scenarios.

Between 2007 and 2009, Sridhar and Chatterji [2,3] developed regression models that

incorporated weather conditions, flight traffic, and airport size to more comprehensively

analyze daily national airspace delays. Klein et al. [4,5] further refined the concept by

proposing the enroute WITI (E-WITI), the terminal area WITI (T-WITI), queuing delays,

and a predictive WITI FA index, and they introduced a scanline-based method (2008–2009)

for assessing sector availability under convective weather forecasts [6,7]. In 2009, Cook

et al. [8] quantified the weather’s effect on air traffic via WITI. Mukherjee et al. [9] used

WITI to classify weather days and compare weather-induced rerouting with National

Advanced Planning Manual guidelines [10] developed a WITI-based method to evaluate

sector capacity under severe weather.

2.1.2. Research on Deep Prediction Models

In recent years, Deep learning has rapidly transformed both weather prediction and

traffic flow prediction. In 2020, Weyn et al. [11] introduced a data-driven global weather

prediction framework using a deep CNN to fuse multichannel weather data, significantly

boosting prediction accuracy. Building on this, Dong Kyun Kim et al. [12] combined Con-

vLSTM with 3D CNN for precipitation nowcasting, demonstrating deep models’ strength

in capturing spatiotemporal dynamics, while Jesús García Fernández et al. [13] proposed

Broad UNet to reduce parameter counts without sacrificing performance in nowcasting. In

2022, Fowdur et al. [14] advanced a real-time collaborative machine learning framework

that integrates distributed data sources to improve situational awareness for convective

weather. More recently, Zhang J et al. [15] developed a CNN BiLSTM attention model that

enhances severe convective weather forecasts, and Jardines et al. [16] created a CNN-based

system for pre-tactical thunderstorm prediction to support flight scheduling. For very

short-term predictions, Bouchard et al. [17] compared threshold methods, neural networks,

and belief functions for lightning strike risk, and Gao J et al. (2023) [18] showed that a hy-

brid multichannel CNN-GRU model yields high precision in ultra-short-term wind power

forecasting. Additionally, GraphCast [19] delivers global daily forecasts up to 10 days with

higher accuracy and much lower cost than NWP; MetNet-2 provides 1 km, 12 h end-to-end

forecasts, showcasing deep learning’s extended lead-time edge [20].

In the traffic flow domain, traditional CNN/LSTM pipelines evolved toward graph

architectures. Wang C et al. [21] introduce the Bo-GRA-CNN-BiLSTM model and employ

Bayesian optimization for automatic hyperparameter tuning to enhance adaptability and

accuracy under varying weather conditions. Cai Kaiquan et al. proposed a Geographical

and Operational GCN, or multi-airport flight delay prediction, effectively capturing spatial–

operational dependencies [22]. Qi et al. [23] proposed STFGCN, which fuses weather and

traffic via spatial–temporal relationship modeling to achieve top performance across all

weather conditions. Beyond Bayesian and Adam optimization methods, genetic algorithms

and other evolutionary strategies are increasingly adopted for hyperparameter tuning in

intelligent transportation systems. For instance, Akopov and Beklaryan [24] proposed a

hybrid clustering-assisted genetic algorithm for the evolutionary synthesis of high-capacity

multilayer road networks. Similarly, Priya and Francis [25] integrated a multi-objective

genetic algorithm with RNNs to enhance traffic speed prediction accuracy in complex urban

environments. Collectively, these advances illustrate deep learning’s power to improve

predictive accuracy and model design, while highlighting ongoing challenges in model

complexity, data heterogeneity, and generalization. 

2.2. Research Gap 

Most existing research relies on statistical data to measure how convective weather

affects traffic operations, mainly analyzing the overall effects in terminal areas. However,

these studies have some important limitations:

(1) Do not take into account the spatial distribution of convective weather in the

terminal area and its relative position to the flight trajectory.

(2) Current methods focus on single-airport or flight-level delay prediction, neglecting

comprehensive evaluation of multi-airport collaborative delay levels under terminal-area

operational scenarios.

(3) Conventional models rely on manually designed static features (e.g., visibility,

wind speed), which inadequately capture spatial correlations between weather patterns

and flight paths, resulting in suboptimal prediction accuracy.

We summarized the above studies in four aspects, including study area, research focus,

key features, and unaddressed gaps, as shown in Table 1.

Therefore, this paper proposes a method for predicting airport delay level based on

deep metric learning. The method focuses on airport delay levels as the main research

object. First, the FCM algorithm is used to cluster delay-related features, extract classi-

fication rules for delay levels, and assign delay level labels. Then, actual data from the

terminal area, including weather avoidance areas (WAF) and radar tracks (ADS-B), are

converted into multi-channel image features that retain spatial location information. After

that, a delay-level prediction model is built using a convolutional neural network. The

aim of this method is to provide a comprehensive and objective way to describe and

monitor the overall operational status of the terminal area. By accurately predicting the

airport’s delay conditions during convective weather, the method aims to support traffic

management decisions in the pre-tactical phase and improve the operational efficiency of

the terminal area.

3. Methodology 

3.1. Multi-Channel Traffic and Weather Scene Image 

Predicting airport delay levels under convective weather is challenging because tra-

ditional numerical features cannot fully capture the complex impact on terminal flight

operations [24]. To address this issue, we apply deep learning by converting air traffic

scene data into multi-channel images, from which useful features can be extracted. Figure 1

shows the overall framework for constructing the Multi-Channel Traffic and Weather Scene

Image (MTWSI).

3.1.1. Data Description

This study uses Weather Advective Flow (WAF) data together with air traffic data,

which are further categorized into dynamic flight data and static airspace information.

WAF data represent convective cloud information captured by weather radar and are used

to predict and monitor extreme weather events (e.g., torrential rains, thunderstorms, and

tornadoes) [25]. Each WAF dataset consists of 1250   ×   1250 data points, with each point

indicating convective weather intensity over a 200 m   ×   200 m area. Static airspace data

includes fixed aeronautical information, such as latitude and longitude, which define the

structure of the airspace. Dynamic flight data, collected via satellite communication or

ADS-B, provides real-time updates on aircraft position, altitude, speed, navigation, and

flight status. In the proposed MTWSI-based deep learning method, the airspace is first

rasterized using the static data. Then, the resulting grid is filled with dynamic flight data

to form detailed traffic and weather scenarios. These images are then used for feature

extraction to predict airport delay levels.

3.1.2. Construction of Images

(1)   Rasterization of terminal areas

Convective weather in the terminal area is a major factor contributing to airport delays,

especially when a high volume of flights is scheduled. In addition, the relative position

between the convective weather and flight paths plays a key role in determining the severity

of the impact. For this reason, this paper proposes a new data representation method called

multi-channel air traffic and weather scene images to portray the impact of convective

weather within the terminal area on flight operations and to serve as an input to airport

delay prediction models. 

Figure 1.   Procedural Framework for Constructing MTWSI. 

Each image is represented as a two-dimensional matrix. Therefore, the target terminal

area is first rasterized to serve as the foundation for subsequent image construction [26].

To ensure consistency and ease of processing, the outer square boundary of the terminal

area is selected as the image extent and divided into 125   ×   125 grids (each 2 km wide) (see

Figure 2). Since each grid cell contains latitude and longitude information, the aircraft’s

location within the airspace can be assigned to the corresponding grid, which allows us to

populate the grid with traffic operation information, such as traffic volume, average flight

altitude, average flight speed, etc. However, because each grid cell can store only one value,

multiple 2D matrices are employed to represent diverse traffic and weather information.

These various 2D matrices together form a multi-channel image that reflects various aspects

of traffic and weather conditions in the same area. By combining these channels, a realistic

picture of the air traffic situation is created.

125   ×   125 terminal area grid.   The Weather Severity Index (WSI) is then calculated for

structed. These include inbound flight volume channel, inbound flight average flight speed

channel, inbound flight average flight altitude channel, departing flight volume channel,

departing flight average flight speed channel, departing flight average flight altitude chan-

tion experiment to screen the channels with various combinations of the channels, and the

image resolution is set to 25   ×   25, and the model performance is evaluated by the Accuracy,

Precision, Recall, and F1 metrics. Given that the severity of convective weather is a key

factor affecting airport delays, all channel combinations must include the three convective

3.2. CNN-Based Airport Delay Level Prediction Model 

Considering image-based methods, we employ a CNN learning strategy to develop

an end-to-end method for airport delay prediction, referred to as ADLCNN. Specifically,

the raw weather and traffic data are first rasterized to construct MTWSIs.   Then, the

CNN extracts useful information from these images through nonlinear transformations,

completing the feature learning process. Finally, the learned features are used to evaluate

the delay level. Figure 5 shows the overall framework of the proposed ADLCNN method.

3.2.1. Classification Method

Airport delay level provides an overall description of various types of delays occurring

at an airport within a specific period. It serves as an important indicator for evaluating

airport operational efficiency. Using a single feature to describe delay can only reflect

one aspect of the situation and fails to capture the full picture of flight delays and their

evolving patterns. Therefore, this paper selects two indicators—the average departure

delay time and the on-time departure rate—to assess the airport departure delay level from

the perspectives of both delay severity and flight reliability.

Airport delay classification can be regarded as an unsupervised learning problem, so

in this paper, we will use the fuzzy C-mean (FCM) clustering [27]. The principle of the

FCM algorithm is to minimize the criterion function through continuous iteration. After

FCM cluster analysis, the flight delay dataset can be divided into different clusters, each

representing a level of delay. Therefore, each cluster can be regarded as a delay level,

which can be used to classify the degree of delay at the airport. Figure 6 demonstrates

the trend of the contour coefficient with the number of clusters, where the number of

clusters of 2 corresponds to the highest contour coefficient, indicating that the samples in

the clustering results are more cohesive and separated at this time. Figure 7 demonstrates

the distribution of different delay features within each cluster when the number of clusters

is 2, in which the distribution of average departure delays between two clusters is relatively

more separated, while there is a staggered distribution of flight punctuality, but the overall

flight punctuality of the samples in Cluster 0 is higher, and the overall flight punctuality of

the samples in Cluster 1 is lower.

congestion and improving decision efficiency.

4. Experiment and Results 

4.1. Experimental Setup 

In this section, we validate the effectiveness of the proposed deep metric learning-

based airport delay prediction method using real-world data. The experiments are con-

ducted with one year (2019) of WAF and air traffic data from Guangzhou Baiyun Airport

(ZGGG). Each sample represents an MTWSI constructed from one-hour segments of WAF

and air traffic data, along with delay level labels derived according to the classification

rules described in Section 3.2. In the following experiments, the dataset is divided into

training and test sets in a 7:3 ratio. Additionally, two sets of comparison experiments are

conducted. The first compares the prediction accuracy of the proposed method with that of

a traditional numerical feature processing approach. The second evaluates how different

separation rates in multi-channel images affect prediction accuracy.

The experiments were performed on a computer equipped with an AMD EPYC 7642

48-core processor, 24 GB RAM, and an NVIDIA RTX A5000 GPU. The Python programming

language, version 3.7.9, was used. Deep learning frameworks TensorFlow 2.4.1 and Keras

2.4.3 were used for model construction and training. Data were processed and analyzed

using the NumPy 1.19.5 and pandas 1.2.1 libraries. Machine learning tasks were imple-

mented using the scikit-learn 0.24.1 library. The results were visualized using Matplotlib

3.3.4 and Seaborn 0.11.1 libraries. The experiments were conducted on Ubuntu 20.04.

The predictive model is evaluated using accuracy, precision, recall, F1 score, and

Matthews correlation coefficient.

The raw dataset is highly unbalanced and significantly affects the performance of the

classifier. To overcome this problem, this study will use sampling techniques to improve

the category distribution and avoid overlap. Sampling techniques include under-sampling,

oversampling, and hybrid methods.   Currently, popular sampling techniques include

the following:

(1) Under-sampling (US): Random Under-Sampling (RUS), Edited Nearest Neighbors

(ENN), and Tomek Links.

(2) Oversampling (OS) [29,30]: Random Over-Sampling (ROS), Synthetic Minority

Over-sampling Technique (SMOTE), and Adaptive Synthetic (ADASYN).

(3) Combination (UOS) [29,30]: SMOTETomek and SMOTEENN

5. Conclusions 

This study introduces a multi-channel, image-based method that automatically cap-

tures complex, weather-affected traffic features to predict airport delay. The proposed

method comprises three main components:

(1) Fuzzy C-Means (FCM) clustering is applied to classify airport departure delay levels.

(2) Weather Advective Flow (WAF) data, combined with incoming and outgoing flight

data, are rasterized to construct a multi-channel image that portrays air traffic operation

scenarios under convective weather conditions.

(3) Deep learning techniques analyze features in the generated images to predict

airport delay levels. Experimental results demonstrate that the proposed method achieves

significantly higher prediction accuracy compared to traditional machine learning methods.

The proposed model significantly enhances the accuracy of delay prediction and

offers effective support for airport operational decisions. It facilitates proactive resource

allocation, schedule adjustments, and reduced passenger waiting times. Furthermore,

the model proves highly applicable in real-world scenarios, particularly in capturing the

impact of complex weather on air traffic.   Its ability to improve operational efficiency

and reduce management costs underscores its strong practical value and potential for

broader adoption.

Next steps could explore these key areas:

(1) Designing more complex and effective networks to further enhance prediction

performance.

(2) Integrating clustering methods with domain knowledge and professional experi-

ence to improve explanatory power in airport delay classification.

(3) Incorporating advanced temporal feature extraction techniques to better capture

the time-dependent characteristics inherent in air traffic scenarios.

(4) Considering the method’s integration with existing operational protocols such as

those of ICAO or local air traffic management systems, the current approach remains at the

research stage and has not yet been embedded in formal procedures.
