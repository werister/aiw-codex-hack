Abstract —One of the most challenging tasks for air traffic

controllers is runway configuration management (RCM). It deals

with   the   optimal   selection   of   runways   on   which   to   operate

(for   arrivals   and   departures)   based   on   current   and   forecast

traffic, surface wind speed, wind direction, other environmental

variables, noise constraints, and several other airport-specific

factors. In this paper, a methodology using supervised learning

is developed to build a predictive model for RCM decision-

support from large volumes of historical data. Data from two full

years (2018 and 2019) related to current and forecast weather,

demand/capacity, etc. is collected, analyzed, and fused together. A

variety of supervised learning algorithms are tested for predicting

runway   configuration,   and   hyperparameter   tuning   is   carried

out to select the best performing model. The validation process

involves two airports of low (Charlotte Douglas International

Airport, CLT) and high (Denver International Airport, DEN)

complexity of configuration decision-making. The results show

significant promise for the two airports with test accuracy of

93% (CLT) and 73% (DEN). The methodology is scalable and

generalizable to other airports across the U.S. National Airspace

System. 

Index Terms —air traffic management, runway configuration

management, machine learning 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

One of the key tenets of the next generation air transporta-

tion system (NextGen) is deploying automation capabilities

through decision-support tools (DST) to aid air traffic con-

trollers. Among such automation capabilities is the application

of data-driven machine learning approaches to solve complex

problems in air traffic management (ATM). One of the most

challenging tasks for air traffic controllers is runway configu-

ration management (RCM). It deals with the optimal selection

of runways on which to operate (for arrivals and departures)

based on traffic, surface wind speed, wind direction, other

environmental variables, noise constraints, and several other

airport-specific factors. RCM affects the efficiency of the Na-

tional Airspace System (NAS) and both surface and airspace

operations can benefit from a robust and accurate prediction

of runway configurations.

There are two main types of analyses around RCM – 

prescriptive   and   predictive   approaches. The main goal of pre-

scriptive approach is to recommend the most optimal runway

configuration for a given situation based on prevailing and/or

forecast meteorological conditions, traffic load, intended sys-

tem goals, and constraints. A sub-optimal selection of the

runway configuration, or poor timing of configuration changes,

can result in increase in taxi times for aircraft on the surface

of the airport, excessive fuel burn due to holding patterns

or increased taxi-times, and go-arounds for arriving traffic

(e.g., due to excessive tail winds). The techniques in this type

of application include heuristic search [1], [2], optimization

and dynamic queuing theory [3]–[7], dynamic programming

[8], and reinforcement learning [9]. Air traffic controllers and

front-line managers are generally the intended stakeholders for

prescriptive models.

Predictive models do not aim to improve existing decision-

making rationale but rather try to learn it from historical data

and mimic it with least amount of error. These models try to

capture existing heuristics typically through supervised learn-

ing models [10]–[13]. Both air traffic management personnel

and operators can benefit from predictive models through

reduction of uncertainty in the prediction of airport capacity,

better management of the traffic flow, and better utilization of

resources. These two family of models are important and have

received significant attention in literature, with more diverse

applications seen in the prescriptive models [14].

Both prescriptive and predictive approaches generally in-

volve building a model using historical operations data. This

model is either based purely on the data (no site-specific adap-

tation required) or requires detailed airport-specific adaptation

(such as a queuing network model [4], [5], [7]). Purely data-

driven models have the advantage of not requiring airport-

specific adaptation and tend to be extensible to other airports.

On   the   other   hand,   a   model   that   takes   into   account   the

dynamics of the airport movement can use simulation to

optimize for the runway configuration selection or prediction.

While both predictive and prescriptive approaches have

their own advantages and limitations as well as different

applicability, this work focuses on predictive models. Recent

literature in runway configuration prediction have used prob-

abilistic approaches [3], [15] and/or supervised learning [12],

[13] using limited number of variables such as the weather

conditions, time of the day, and time of year. Other studies

have developed a recursive multi-step forecasting model using

supervised   learning   to   predict   the   best   choice   of   runway

configuration given all the factors such as weather and future

traffic [10]. Such methods use a vast amount of historical

data to imitate the decision-making made by the controller

in the past with least amount of error. Other approaches

have attempted to predict the arrival and departure runway

assignments rather than airport configurations [11]. While

these approaches present promise in the predictive setting,

there is room for improvement in the prediction accuracy by

incorporating other relevant features and/or developing more

capable models.

There are several challenges associated with RCM pre-

dictive models due to which it is the subject of ongoing

research. Air traffic controllers set the runway configuration

based on relevant information and forecasting available at the

time, including weather, traffic, noise abatement procedures,

and safety bounds. This makes the process subjective due to

potential biases in human decision-making as the same or

similar conditions can lead to a different action by a different

controller. There is also the challenge of inherent uncertainty

in   the   accuracy   of   the   available   information,   particularly

weather forecast models and demand forecasts. An automated

approach based on data mining can make use of the abundance

of available historical data/decisions and learn through many

more possible scenarios under uncertainty to provide the best

estimate of runway configuration in the future.

In this paper, we present a comprehensive implementation

of predictive models for runway configuration prediction from

large volumes of historical data. Specifically, operational data

from two full years (2018 and 2019) are collected, analyzed,

and fused together to build the data product used in this

work. The data set differs from prior work in the literature in

terms of its scope, resolution, and variety of factors collected

and considered. Meteorological data is collected from two

different sources: current weather conditions from METAR

(METeorological Aerodrome Reports) and forecast weather

conditions from Localized Aviation Model Output Statistics

(MOS) Program (LAMP). Operational data from the Federal

Aviation Administration (FAA) Aviation System Performance

Metrics (ASPM) related to scheduled and actual number of

arrivals and departures, and average taxi time are collected.

NASA’s Sherlock Data Warehouse is used to identify other

critical information such as go-arounds, and other events that

might impact RCM decision-making. The overall goal is to

learn, as closely as possible, decision-making in the historical

data to predict for runway configuration for the future.

A. Data Sources Identification and Extraction 

Four main data sources are used in this work, based on a

survey of prior work. The data has been obtained from these

sources and fused together using GMT time. The following

subsections describe details of each data source utilized, the

characteristics of the data, and the specific fields from the

data that are used in this work. The four sources of data are:

(1) METAR (METeorological Aerodrome Reports)

(2) LAMP (Localized Aviation MOS Program)

(3) Sherlock Data Warehouse

(4) ASPM (Aviation System Performance Metrics).

Two types of meteorological data are used in this work

which provide information about current weather conditions

and forecast weather over the next several hours. 

1)   METAR :   Prevailing weather conditions at an airport are

available through the METeorological Aerodrome Reports

.

Weather   stations   from   around   the   world   report   weather

available   in   the   database   includes:   temperature,   dewpoint,

wind speed, wind direction, visibility, clouds and ceiling, and

any reported weather conditions. 

2)   LAMP :   Weather forecast data is obtained from the

LAMP system. LAMP is a statistical system that provides

and   Atmospheric   Administration   (NOAA)   Weather   and

forecast   guidance   on   the   NDFD   continental   U.S.   2.5-km

grid   out   to   25   hours

for the following weather elements: temperature, dewpoint,

configurations for use as labels in a machine learning model.

small instance of cases, these reported configurations were

found to be inaccurate for various reasons. Using the data of

each of the individual flights that landed during a time period

provides the highest level of fidelity around which runways

or configurations were actually used.

B. Data Processing and Transformation 

From among the data sources identified, there are several

parameters available for use in the machine learning models. 

===== PDF PAGE 4 / 9 =====

The first step is to identify and retain any parameter that

might   impact   runway   configuration   decision-making   and

discard the other parameters. This selection is a conservative

filtering   and   many   of   the   parameters   are   retained.   The

next   step   is   to   identify   and   mitigate   data   quality   issues,

perform   data   transformations,   and   convert   text   data   into

numeric   values.   Data   transformation   is   an   important   step

as   it translates the   raw   data   into   features and   labels for

use in a machine learning model. Data processing involves

several key steps such as aggregation of data to the frequency

being considered (15-minutes intervals in this work), filling

missing data, and encoding categorical variables. Variables

deemed important, but that are unavailable or missing in

most of the data need to be excluded. Data processing and

transformation thus convert the raw data from various sources

into features that can be used for machine learning. Through

the data processing and transformation step, each 15-minute

interval in the data available   is   associated with a set of

features and a unique runway configuration. The following

two subsections identify the features used in the final model

(including the rationale) and the labels for supervised learning. 

1) Features:   Table II shows various features used in the

machine learning (ML) model for predicting runway config-

uration. The table contains the feature name, the data source

used for building this feature, and the feature type (categorical,

continuous). The first set of features are derived from the

METAR data. The wind speed is a continuous feature and

wind direction is discretized into eight distinct categories,

which are then encoded using one-hot encoding. Wind speed

and direction are important variables that affect the airport

configuration as high tailwinds and crosswinds are opera-

tionally unsafe and can render certain configurations unusable.

Temperature, ceiling, and visibility are other weather features

that are included from the METAR data and are continuous

variables. The meteorological condition is a binary variable

having two possible states: Visual Meteorological Conditions

(VMC) and Instrument Meteorological Conditions (IMC). It

indicates the prevailing conditions based on the combination

of visibility and the cloud ceiling.

The next set of features are those derived from the ASPM

data. This includes the scheduled arrivals and departures,

which give a sense of the demand at the airport. Demand is an

important factor that affects possible airport configurations as

high-demand situations would require high-capacity configu-

rations to be used. The capacity of arrivals and departures is

the next feature used from ASPM and it provides information

about the number of arrivals that a configuration can ac-

commodate. Capacity is also typically correlated with various

weather conditions (particularly VMC versus IMC). ASPM

also contains the count of actual arrivals and departures,

however, those are not used in this work. Similarly, averaged

taxi-in and taxi-out times are also available from ASPM but

these are not used because they are an aggregated metric that

would typically not be available in real time during operations.

The scheduled arrivals and departures over a 1-hour window

following the current time interval are generated and used as

features rather than the scheduled operations in the current

time interval. The local hour and quarter hour is also available

and is encoded as one-hot categorical variables in order to

account for variations during the day.

Two features are created from event data extracted from

the Sherlock data warehouse: the total number of go-arounds

in a time period and the presence of multiple go-arounds in

a time period. This could be an important feature, because

high tailwinds or crosswinds could be one of the reasons

for a go-around and one or more go-arounds could prompt

the controller to change the runway configuration earlier than

planned.

The final set of features is obtained from the LAMP mete-

orological forecast data. LAMP features contain forecasts for

wind speed, wind direction, ceiling, visibility, temperature, and

meteorological condition. Additionally, LAMP also provides

forecasts of precipitation, convective weather, and lightning

as categorical variables whose values range from 0 to 4,

with increasing numerical value indicating higher probability.

Forecast weather elements are an important part of predictive

runway configuration models, as they can provide insight

into future weather changes and potential for switches in

configurations. Inclement weather forecast can lead to reduced

capacity and changes in schedule as well as the exclusion of

certain runway configurations. The LAMP data features help

account for these scenarios and other implicit decision-making

strategies that may be employed by controllers when they

review the forecast. Since the LAMP data is available at an

hourly interval, the data product in this work will utilize the

most recently available forecast for each 15-minute interval.

While the forecast data is available up to 25 hours in the

future, only the next 2 hours are currently utilized in the ML 

===== PDF PAGE 5 / 9 =====

model, as these are seen as the most relevant to the prediction

of configurations in the immediate next 15-minutes interval.

As is evident from the description of the features as well

as their impact on the runway configuration management

problem,   there   exist   correlations   and   coupling   between

different features used in the ML models. Dimensionality

reduction techniques such as Principal Component Analysis

(PCA) are explored in the implementation section to improve

performance. 

2) Labels/Targets:   The labels or targets for the machine

learning model are the unique runway configurations for each

airport. Thus, CLT has 2 unique labels (N or S) and DEN

has 11 unique labels (identified in Table I). The runway

configuration   in   the   next   15-minutes   time   interval   is   the

label associated with the current interval. In this way, during

operations, the model is using features available at the current

time to make the prediction for the future without any leakage

of data. 

C. ML Model Training, Validation, Testing 

The end product generated from data processing and trans-

formation is used to setup and train the machine learning

models. The overall setup of the model is shown in Figure 3.

At   any   point   in   time,   the   model   uses   features   from   the

current step to predict the runway configuration for the next

step (next 15-minutes interval). Once trained, the model runs

continuously to provide predictions for the next time step

(dashed box and arrows). It is noted here that features in

the current time step might include future estimates of some

quantities (such as weather forecast from LAMP), but these

data are available or collected at the current time step or earlier. 

Fig. 3.   High-level schematic of the methodology. 

Several   different   machine   learning   classification   models

were considered as candidate models for this work including

Random Forest, Gradient Boosting, Support Vector Machine,

etc. Among these models considered, Random Forest had the

best overall performance at both airports and therefore, in

this paper, results from the best performing (after tuning the

hyperparameters) Random Forest model are shown. Standard

machine learning practice is used for training, validation,

and testing of the models. The overall data set is randomly

divided into two sets for training (80%) and testing (20%).

The model hyperparameters are tuned using an off-the-shelf

python package called   GridSearchCV 
5   
that finds the optimal

hyperparameters and performs 5-fold cross validation based

on the training set. For the Random Forest algorithm, the

following hyperparameters are varied   based on   review   of

literature [16] and prior usage of this algorithm – the number

of estimators, maximum depth, minimum number of samples

per leaf. For details on each of the hyperparameters and their

impact on the model, readers are referred to the documentation

of Random Forest model implementation used in this work

(from the python library scikit-learn 
6 
)

III. R ESULTS AND   D ISCUSSION 

In this section, the described methodology is implemented

and the results are reported for the two airports of interest

(CLT and DEN). 

A. Data Description and Exploration 

The data used in this work is collected from the data sources

described earlier for two full years (2018 and 2019) at CLT

and DEN airports. The data product is created using data

processing and transformation to produce one row for each

15-minutes interval during the two years. The sources that are

available at a lower frequency are up-sampled by using data

from the closest past instance. Thus, each row in the final

data product is one instance of the 15-minutes interval and the

columns are the features identified from previous sections. The

final data product is stored and used as a   DataFrame   object in

the pandas library in python 
7 
. The DataFrame for each of the

two airports consists of 70,056 rows. For each airport, 69,480

rows ( ≈   99.1%) do not contain any missing data or features

and are thus used in the model training, validation, and testing.

The total number of columns available from the raw data set

is 212, however, only a subset of these columns are used as

features in the ML model. A total of   84   features are used in the

final ML models in this work. These   84   features correspond

to   30   variables derived from Table II (the higher number of

features than variables is due to the encoding of categorical

features that leads to multiple features for each variable). The

data is then divided into training (80%) and testing (20%)

sets based on random splits. In each of the following sections,

the scores or metrics are all reported on the test set unless

explicitly stated otherwise.

each data set (CLT and DEN) to obtain the best performing

models. Typically, grid search will select the best hyperparam-

main hyperparameters in the model, and it controls the number

of decision trees in the forest. The next hyperparameter is

maximum depth of a decision tree. It controls how far down

the nodes are expanded for each tree. The third hyperparameter

is the max samples. It controls the maximum number of

samples to draw to train each decision tree. Values lower

than one (the max value for this hyperparameter) ensure that

the different decision trees trained have different strengths

that can help with model performance. All three of these

hyperparameters help to achieve the desired trade-off between

model performance and generalizability. It is noted that the

optimal values of the hyperparameters can vary when the data

is changed, even for the same airport. The values provided in

the table gave the best performance for the available data.

For CLT, the model does a good job of identifying the right

preferred in the operations and is thus better represented in

of DEN, it can be seen that a majority of the disagreements are

among very similar configurations. For example, in the case

of S/S configuration, majority of the times that the ML model

disagrees with the historical decisions is in prediction of SE/SE

instead of S/S, which are very similar configurations. Another

example is the confusion of the algorithm between NE/NE

Additionally, for ensuring appropriate behavior in edge-

cases, a metric for violation of obvious decisions is defined.

For this metric, decisions that are obvious to the subject matter

experts are defined as cases. Then, the percentage of times the

model violates these defined cases is identified. For example,

in the case study of CLT, if the wind is blowing strongly (more

than 15 knots) from North, if the model suggests the South

configuration, it is considered a violation. For both models,

very low percentages of violation metric (below 0.07 %   and

0.7 %   for CLT and DEN) are observed.

C. Discussion 

It is observed that the Random Forest model generally

performs well for both CLT and DEN airports. Based on

the metrics and feature importances, it is evident that the

performance of the model depends on the airport complexity

and operational constraints at each airport. The benefits of 

===== PDF PAGE 8 / 9 =====

Fig. 7.   Feature importance for DEN model. 

using a large number of features is evident from the two

feature importance figures where the most important features

are quite different across two airports. As the methodology

is expanded to other airports with more diverse operating

conditions, other features might play an elevated role. The

data sources used in this work are easily available for a

majority of large airports across the NAS and therefore the

methodology lends itself easily to expansion at other airports.

The extraction of labels (actual runway configurations in use)

is an important and time-consuming step that was essential

to ensure most accurate information. Performing this at each

airport might require some amount of initial offline work.

Possible alternative data sources or streams might need to be

explored to scale this methodology. Additionally, the drop in

performance of the model without performing this step can

be explored to gauge the quantitative benefits of doing this

step. This will enable making a decision on whether there is

added value in performing this time-consuming offline analy-

sis. Hyperparameter tuning, while important only marginally

improves a baseline model built with default hyperparameters

from scikit-learn. This indicates that the model is performing

as well as it can with the features that have been provided to it.

Further improvement might require a different model type or

additional features that cover other aspects of operations. Fi-

nally, it was observed that there is a variability in the historical

decisions by controllers when the system is in the exact same

state (weather, forecast, traffic, etc.). This variability can be

captured using a measure known as entropy from information

theory. Entropy allows quantifying how much variability exists

in the controllers’ decisions in any given state and thereby

provides some context on the supervised learning model’s

predictions.

IV. C ONCLUSION 

In this paper, a methodology for predicting airport runway

configurations using machine learning algorithms trained on

data collected from various sources was demonstrated. A

detailed description of data sources and processing required

for the methodology was provided along with a description

for obtaining labels for the ML model. Various ML models

were explored and the best performing algorithms and hyper-

parameters were reported for two representative airports of low

and high operational complexity (CLT and DEN respectively).

There are some identified avenues of future work that would

improve the methodology further. The first avenue identified

is the inclusion of other weather forecast data called Terminal

Aerodrome Forecast (TAF). This is a popular aviation weather

data that is frequently used by air traffic personnel in the

decision-making process. The comparison of using LAMP

versus TAF data will be explored. The second avenue is

expansion of the methodology to other airports. This would

enable learning how the methodology scales and identify gaps

in the current data or model setup, which might help models

at existing airports as well as new ones. The third avenue

of future work is exploring more complex machine learning

models such as deep learning or long short-term memory

(LSTM) networks that can explicitly account for the temporal

nature of the data and potentially help realize additional gains

in the model performance.
